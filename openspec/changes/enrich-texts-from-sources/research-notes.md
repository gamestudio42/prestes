# Research notes (enrich-texts-from-sources)

These are working notes for the apply phase. They paraphrase the sources,
except for the short quotations of the manifesto (GNU FDL) and of Moreira
Lima (public domain). Nothing here reproduces Rosa, Amado, Mozzer, Anita
Prestes or the podcast verbatim beyond a few words.

## manifesto-1925: Manifesto of Porto Nacional

- **Source:** the marxists.org transcription. It is read here through the
  Wayback Machine copy, because marxists.org does not open from this
  machine.
- **Page metadata:** source "Franklin Martins – Conexão Política";
  transcription by Fernando A. S. Araújo; included 08/11/2010; licence GNU
  FDL.
- **Signed** "Porto Nacional, 19 de outubro de 1925" by General Miguel
  Costa, Coronel Luís Carlos Prestes and Coronel Juarez Távora.
- **Addressed** to "Concidadãos" and then "Povo Brasileiro!".
- **Argument, paragraph by paragraph:**
  1. After fifteen months of fighting, the column has reached the heart of
     Brazil, on the Tocantins. It reaffirms that the patriotic crusade
     begun on 5 July in São Paulo, and joined by the gaúchos, has not
     ended.
  2. The column keeps its faith. It holds that most Brazilians want the
     country restored to the liberal principles of the Constitution. That
     Constitution is trampled by a clique of unscrupulous politicians.
  3. The soldiers will not lay down the flag of Liberty while despotism
     lasts.
  4. The column admits that the country, and the people above all, suffer
     the violence that war brings.
  5. It asserts that retreat would betray the dead.
  6. It is not making war out of whim or ambition. It wants peace, but a
     peace without dishonour, founded on justice.
  7. It rejects "a paz sombria e trágica que encobre o vilipêndio das
     senzalas". It would rather be crushed.
- **Short quotations for the game:**
  - "os soldados revolucionários não enrolarão a bandeira da Liberdade"
  - "queremos a paz e não é senão por ela que, há mais de 15 meses, nos
    batemos"
  - "uma paz sem opróbrios, cimentada na justiça"

## mozzer-2009: Marcelo Luiz Cesar Mozzer (Ufes)

"Presença da Coluna Prestes nas veredas do Grande sertão", *Contexto*
no. 15/16 (2008/2009), pp. 246–259. The local PDF is in the owner's
Downloads folder and is not committed.

- **Approach:** a literary reading. The author "intuits" that Rosa drew on
  the column (p. 249) and does not claim proof.
- **Riobaldo's passage** (Rosa 1986, p. 82; also Starling 1999, p. 29), in
  paraphrase: Prestes's soldiers came from Goiás and demanded all the saddle
  animals. There was firing at the mouth of the Urucuia, at São Romão, where
  a government steamer landed troops from Bahia. Years later a farmer
  splitting a log finds bullets lodged in it.
- **Command at Porto Nacional,** October 1925, from Anita Prestes 1995,
  p. 74. Among those named: Miguel Costa, Prestes, Juarez Távora, João
  Alberto, Siqueira Campos, Djalma Dutra, Cordeiro de Farias and others.
- **Patriotic battalions in Bahia,** early 1926 (Anita Prestes 1991, p. 262,
  citing Eul-Soo Pang): about ten in all. The main ones were Horácio de
  Mattos (Lavras Diamantinas, about 1,500), Franklin Lins de Albuquerque
  (middle São Francisco, about 800) and Abílio Wolney (jagunços of Barreiras
  and Goiás, about 1,000). Local chiefs received money and arms from the
  government.
- **Vivandeiras** (Veiga 1992, p. 30): about 1,000 men, 500 horses and 50
  women entered Santa Catarina. The women crossed the river against
  Prestes's order, and he then let them stay.
- **Requisitions** (Veiga 1992, p. 41): there were early abuses. The
  command banned indiscriminate looting, and there were expulsions for
  disobedience.
- **Justice acts** (Anita Prestes 1995, p. 81): the column burned tax
  registers, freed prisoners and destroyed instruments of torture.
- **Moreira Lima:** lawyer, captain and secretary. Amado calls him the
  "Bacharel Feroz" (Amado 1985, p. 191). Mozzer suggests Rosa's "Dr. Meigo
  de Lima" echoes his name.
- **Tia Maria:** Amado (p. 123) has her as the "feiticeira da Coluna", who
  died under torture.
- **Rivers shared by the novel and the route** (Amado p. 131; Bastos 1946;
  Viggiano 1993): Preto, Urucuia, Carinhanha, São Francisco, do Sono,
  Canabrava, Paracatu.
- **Moreira Lima's farewell at La Gaiba** (Lima 1979, p. 523; quoted by
  Mozzer on p. 257). It is public domain, so it can be quoted. Opening:
  "Soldados da Liberdade! Dormi tranqüilos na terra estrangeira que vos
  acolheu com tanta nobreza, porque os vossos nomes e os vossos feitos serão
  eternos no coração generoso do Brasil…"

## historiafm-2024: História FM, episode 190

"Coluna Prestes: a maior marcha militar da história". Host Icles Rodrigues;
guest Amilcar Guidolim Vitor (PhD UFSM; IFFar Santo Ângelo; author of
`vitor-2011`). Published on YouTube on 15 Dec. 2024, 1 h 38 min. Consulted
in full through YouTube's automatic Portuguese transcript, which is error
prone on names. Timestamps are approximate.

- **The motto.** The episode title has "a maior marcha militar da história"
  in distance terms. The guest (~1:19:30) says it is "considered one of the
  greatest revolutionary marches in human history": about 25,000 km in a
  little over two years.
- **Start date** (~51:30). The guest holds that the column began at São
  Luiz Gonzaga on 27 Dec. 1924, following Anita Prestes's thesis (1989).
  Others date it to the union in Paraná in April 1925. Log this in
  TIMELINE; Macaulay is the baseline.
- **São Luiz Gonzaga.** It was chosen because no railway branch reached it.
  The government sent about 14,000 men against 1,000–1,500 rebels; many had
  only pistols, and ammunition was short. Prestes feinted a detachment
  toward São Miguel das Missões and slipped out through the gaps. This was
  the war of movement.
- **Bozano** (Santa Maria intendant, Borges's possible successor) was
  killed in an ambush. Ramada on 3 Jan. 1925 was the hardest fight in the
  RS. Portela died at the Santa Catarina border. At Maria Preta, two
  government detachments fired on each other.
- **Klinger** chased the column with trucks while it cut trails through the
  bush. He wrote two letters to the command proposing an armistice.
- **Porto Nacional.** It is the place of the famous photograph of the
  command, seated and standing. They look older but were very young:
  Prestes was 26. They were received by the Dominican friars (the
  transcript gives the friar's name as "José Aldrin"; probably Frei José M.
  Audrin; verify before use).
- **Justice acts.** Along the march the column burned tax-collection books
  and freed people held in conditions like slavery. In jails it found Black
  men in gargalheiras (iron neck collars) and tied to the tronco. It
  destroyed palmatórias (school paddles) and other instruments of torture.
- **Requisitions.** The column was supplied by no one and had to requisition
  food, horses and clothing. The command issued requisition notes. A
  requisitioned ox or horse could ruin a poor family, and the command came
  to see this. It is one of the reasons for the exile. Government troops
  also looted and raped. The command punished violations, including with
  executions. Government propaganda called the rebels bandits, murderers
  and rapists, and in many places people fled their villages.
- **Size.** The column never exceeded about 1,500. At Teresina, rumour put
  it at 5,000–10,000.
- **Piancó.** Padre Aristides Ferreira da Cruz, a federal deputy, had white
  flags raised and then opened fire. He was executed afterwards, and
  government propaganda used his death.
- **Lampião** received a captain's patent from the government through Padre
  Cícero. He never met the column.
- **Cleto Campelo and the PCB.** Cleto Campelo and PCB men in Recife sent
  emissaries. This was the column's only contact with the Communist Party.
  Prestes was not a communist in the 1920s.
- **Mariante's "grupos de caça"** in Bahia mixed civilians and soldiers.
- **Back in Mato Grosso** there were about 800 men. About 600 entered
  Bolivia, among them women and children. The guest says "February 1926",
  a slip for 1927. Some went on to Argentina and others to Paraguay.
  Prestes stayed and found work for the men with an English company.
- **1928:** the paper *A Esquerda* marks Prestes's 30th birthday and calls
  him the "Cavaleiro da Esperança".
- **Women** (~1:24–1:28):
  - About 50 in all: about 30 from the RS and about 20 who came with the
    São Paulo troops.
  - At the Uruguay river, Prestes ordered that they not cross; Siqueira
    Campos was firmly against them. They crossed the night before and were
    waiting on the far bank. Prestes was outvoted.
  - Moreira Lima's diary reduces their role to cooks and companions. In
    fact they fought, carried the wounded and gathered information.
  - Most are known only by nicknames, which hides them: Santa Rosa,
    Chininha, Cara de Macaca, Tia Maria.
  - Santa Rosa gave birth in Mato Grosso and rode on with the baby within
    hours.
  - Government troops made Tia Maria dig her own grave before executing
    her, in the Northeast.
  - Hermínia was an Austrian nurse who kept her name.
  - The thesis cited is Maria Meire de Carvalho (`carvalho-2001`).
- **Black soldiers.** A large share of the rank and file was Black, hidden
  by labels such as "caboclos" and "pelo-duro". There is no count. There
  were Black leaders in the command too. A 1984 photograph shows Prestes at
  Santo Ângelo with elderly veterans, many of them Black.
- **Memory.** The first memorial is Santo Ângelo (1996), with a Niemeyer
  monument. Others are Santa Helena (PR), Arraias and the Tocantins memorial
  (2001). The centenary was marked in 2024.

## Tenentismo and positivism (added 2026-11-12 at the owner's request)

### macaulay-1974 (Tier 0, local text layer)

- **Ch. I, pp. 4–5: who the Tenentes were.**
  - "Tenentes" was the common name for the young army rebels.
  - In 1924 they found a retired senior officer, Isidoro Dias Lopes, to
    head the movement.
  - Their demands: Bernardes to resign; a provisional government; a
    convention for a new constitution.
- **Ch. I, p. 5: why they hated Bernardes.**
  - Minas Gerais and São Paulo, acting together, named the president. The
    1922 deal was Bernardes now, São Paulo's choice in 1926.
  - The officer corps came mostly from the weaker states, the Federal
    District and the city of São Paulo, and resented the deal.
  - Some officers shared urban middle-class aspirations. All had been
    taught at school to serve the nation, not regional interests, and they
    identified those interests with Bernardes.
  - Their dislike of him turned into hatred during the 1922 campaign.
  - Officers backed Nilo Peçanha, the 1921 candidate of Pernambuco, Bahia,
    Rio de Janeiro and the Rio Grande do Sul.
- **Ch. II, pp. 33–34: positivism in Prestes's own family and schooling.**
  - Prestes's father took part, as a cadet, in the 1889 coup. He was a
    fervent republican and a follower of Auguste Comte.
  - The academy was then at Praia Vermelha. Its cadets rioted in 1895 and
    rose in the 1904 vaccine revolt, which was partly fed by Comte's
    hostility to Jenner's vaccine.
  - The government then moved the academy to Realengo, 25 km from the
    centre. When Prestes enrolled in 1916, "Comte's elitist dogma" and a
    spirit of rebellion still haunted it.
- **Ch. III, pp. 64–66: Benjamin Constant and Rondon.**
  - Benjamin Constant Botelho de Magalhães taught Comte's "order and
    progress" to the cadets of the 1880s. The doctrine held that society
    improves by scientific laws, and that discrimination by religion,
    heredity or race marks backward societies.
  - Brazil, with its monarchy, its established church and its slavery,
    broke those laws. Professor and cadets, Rondon among them, campaigned
    against slavery.
  - On 15 Nov. 1889 they gave the monarchy "a slight shove". The republic
    separated church and state and put the positivist motto "Ordem e
    Progresso" on the new flag.
  - Comte's ideal was a "sociocracy" of virtuous men. It was not a
    democracy. It would protect the weak and give free, compulsory
    education, but it was to be reached by moral evolution, never by
    revolution.
  - Rondon remained an orthodox positivist, convinced that force produces
    no social good. The cadets "ignored the master's injunction against
    revolution".
  - The irony for the game: Rondon, the orthodox positivist, commanded the
    government forces against the rebels in Paraná (ch. III, "The
    Revolution versus Rondon").

### historiafm-2024

- **(~6:00–12:00) The army after the Paraguayan War.**
  - Positivism dominated the Escola Militar da Praia Vermelha through
    Benjamin Constant.
  - The army came out for abolition and made the 1889 coup.
  - Officers saw themselves as the "regenerators of the Republic".
  - The doctrine of the "soldado-cidadão" (José Murilo de Carvalho) held
    that soldiers may, and should, act in politics.
  - After 1904 came the push to professionalize the army: Hermes da
    Fonseca, the "jovens turcos" trained in Germany, compulsory service,
    the Realengo school (1913), the review *A Defesa Nacional* (1913) and
    the French military mission (1920s).
- **(~12:00–18:00) The road to 1922.**
  - The civilian war minister Pandiá Calógeras, and pay disputes.
  - The "cartas falsas" (Correio da Manhã, Oct. 1921): letters attributed to
    Bernardes insulting the army and Hermes. They were later proven
    forgeries, but the damage was done.
  - The Reação Republicana and Nilo Peçanha.
  - The 1922 election, with fraud.
  - The arrest of Hermes and the closing of the Clube Militar, which led to
    the 5 July revolt.
- **(~18:00) What tenentismo was.**
  - A political-military movement of the junior officers (lieutenants and
    captains), while the high command stayed loyal.
  - Its members were young Realengo graduates from the urban middle layers.
  - In 1922 it had no programme beyond removing the president. Over time it
    built liberal banners: the secret ballot, political morality, education.
- **(~21:00) Three historiographical readings.**
  - Middle-class revolt: Virgínio Santa Rosa (1930s), Sodré, Carone, Abguar
    Bastos.
  - The army as an institution: Boris Fausto, Paulo Sérgio Pinheiro (the
    transcript garbles the names), Decio Saes, José Augusto Drummond.
  - A combination of both: Maria Cecília Spina Forjaz, Anna Maria Martinez
    Corrêa, Anita Prestes.
- **(~34:00, 1:21:00) The break.** In the May 1930 manifesto Prestes breaks
  with the tenentes. Many of them (Miguel Costa, João Alberto, Cordeiro de
  Farias, Juarez Távora) go with Vargas.

## Macaulay on Porto Nacional (checked for task 1.3)

Macaulay, ch. VI "North Toward the Northeast", pp. 163–166:

- **Arrival.** On the way in, the prior of the Dominicans, Friar José M.
  Audrin (French, 46), promised no resistance and invited the officers to
  the monastery. The chiefs asked him to tell people not to leave their
  homes. Most fled anyway.
- **The town.** Fewer than 6,000 people, the urban centre of northern Goiás,
  with a bishop, a convent school and the newspaper _Norte de Goiás_ on an
  1860 press.
- **Dates.** Advance elements arrived on 12 Oct.; headquarters moved into
  the monastery on 16 Oct.; the column left on 22 Oct., by raft across the
  Tocantins.
- **Conduct.** Audrin said mass and baptized the camp followers' babies. The
  rebels mostly refrained from looting, and some refugees came back. People
  asked to see "Princess Isabela"; the only Isabela turned out to be a camp
  follower nicknamed "Pisca-Pisca".
- **O Libertador.** Moreira Lima, Dr. José Damião Pinheiro Machado and
  Padre Manuel de Macedo printed issue no. 7 on the _Norte de Goiás_ press.
  The first six had been printed by Pinheiro Machado at São Luís (RS) in
  late 1924. The paper's purpose was to refute the charge that the rebels
  were common criminals.
- **The jail.** The rebels paroled the convicts, among them an old Black man
  serving thirty years despite a jury's acquittal (the facts as Moreira Lima
  found them). They removed or destroyed the stocks, fetters and ferules.
  Receipts were given for requisitioned property.
- **Audrin's letter.** After a week he wrote that the passage was a
  lamentable disaster and that the poor were reduced to misery. He offered
  to mediate, and the rebels accepted without conditions. Talks failed over
  what "understanding" meant.
- **Audrin's verdict.** He judged the rebels idealistic but "essentially
  malign" in their passage: freeing criminals, tearing up jails, burning
  records. He said "The Column of Death" suited them better than
  "Unvanquished Column".
- **The manifesto.** Macaulay does not mention the 19 Oct. manifesto. Its
  date and place are the document's own, and they fit the stay of 12–22 Oct.
