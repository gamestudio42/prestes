# Forces

Who fought, what each force was called and how it was armed, for the game's
armies and section types (`faction-armies`). Paraphrased notes from
`macaulay-1974` (Tier 0); nothing here is transcribed. Page numbers are the
book's printed pages, taken from the text layer (PDF page = book page + 16)
and checked against the running heads; the text layer has OCR errors, so
verify against the page image before quoting.

Confidence: **high** = stated outright by Macaulay; **medium** = stated in
passing or for one episode only; **low** = an inference from several
passages.

## Terms

- Macaulay calls the full-time, militarized constabularies of the states
  **militia** and their men **militiamen**; part-time soldiers in government
  service are **volunteers** or **provisionals** (author's note at the end of
  the Foreword). In these notes "state militia" means the same thing: the
  state's own armed force, which is both its police and its army.
- **Loyalist** and **legalist** are used alike for the government side.

## Did the police fight for the government?

Asked by the owner on 2026-11-11. From Macaulay: **yes, and they were a
large part of the government's strength; but in São Paulo they stood on
both sides.**

- The states' own forces (which Macaulay calls militias, and which were
  each state's police and army at once) outnumbered the federal army in
  1925, and the theatre commander preferred them in the front line (pp. 71,
  90). Militias of Rio Grande do Sul, Santa Catarina, Minas Gerais, São
  Paulo, Bahia, Goiás and Piauí all appear against the rebels (table above).
- In the São Paulo revolt of July 1924 the state militia split. Its cavalry
  regiment went over to the revolt, arranged beforehand by its executive
  officer, Major Miguel Costa; a militia battalion that had joined switched
  back to the government; about 1,500 loyal policemen, firemen and
  militiamen held the governor's mansion (pp. 9–10, 19). Two hundred and
  twenty rebels who surrendered on the Paraná that September were
  professionals of the São Paulo militia (p. 24), and Miguel Costa, the
  division's commander, was a former major of it (pp. 85, 91).

For the game: a state militia unit is a legalist regular; a São Paulo
militia veteran can also stand in the column's ranks, which is where the
column's formal commander came from.

## Lampião and the Patriotic Battalion

For the Cangaceiros section type (the owner's decision of 2026-11-11).
Macaulay, p. 191, confidence high: the leader of a Patriotic Battalion in
Ceará invited the bandit Lampião to join it; Lampião reached Juazeiro with
forty-nine men on 4 March 1926, was given a paper commissioning him a
captain of the army reserve, exchanged his men's Winchesters for new army
Mausers and rode out on 7 March, supposedly against the column. By
Macaulay's account the cangaceiros soon gave up any such intention and went
back to banditry, and the theatre commander had no control over them
(p. 192). Macaulay distinguishes the cangaceiro, a full-time roving bandit,
from the jagunço, a part-time gunman called to arms by his patron.

For the game: the commissioning is historical; a cangaceiro section fighting
the column is **dramatized** and labelled so. The section names no person.
