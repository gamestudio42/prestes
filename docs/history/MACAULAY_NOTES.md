## Facts relevant to the vertical slice

### 1922 — the revolt Prestes missed

- Prestes was a young engineering officer, second-in-command of the First
  Engineer Company at Deodoro, when the 1922 conspiracy was organized. The plan
  included cutting telegraph and telephone lines into Rio while cadets marched
  from Realengo and Marshal Hermes da Fonseca took command at Vila Militar.
- Prestes contracted typhoid fever on 13 June 1922. He was still bedridden at
  his mother's house on the night of 4 July, tried to dress, and collapsed. The
  revolt went forward without him and failed. The Realengo cadets were fired on
  at Vila Militar. (Chapter II, book pp. 37–38.)
- The Forte de Copacabana fight is **chapter I, book pp. 26–29**, not chapter
  II. The fort held twenty-nine revolutionaries after the commander was taken
  prisoner; twenty-eight marched out on the afternoon of 6 July. Four deserted
  on the avenue; sixteen met the government troops; eleven held the sidewalk and
  four took cover in construction pipes. Seven rebels were killed or died of
  wounds; Siqueira Campos, one other officer and one enlisted man survived.
  Government losses were thirty-five killed or wounded. The "Eighteen of the
  Fort" is the legend, not the head count. (Chapter I, book pp. 26–29.)
- This confirms the design decision: the Prologue is played as Siqueira Campos
  and the rebels at the Fort, not as Prestes. The game may use the legend of the
  eighteen, but it must not present eighteen as the historical strength of the
  sortie.

### 1923 — Rio Grande do Sul

- The state civil war pitted Borges de Medeiros' followers (chimangos) against
  the Liberator Alliance (maragatos), led politically by Joaquim Francisco de
  Assis Brasil. A federal-mediated settlement kept Borges in office for a final
  term but barred his reelection; the alliance was disarmed and radicalized.
  (Chapter I, book pp. 7–8.)

### 1924 — São Paulo

- The São Paulo revolt began on 5 July 1924, the second anniversary of the 1922
  Rio revolt. Young officers (tenentes), including veterans of 1922, brought in
  retired colonel Isidoro Dias Lopes and promoted him to general.
- The rebels demanded President Artur Bernardes' resignation and a provisional
  government that would convoke a constitutional convention. Garrisons in Mato
  Grosso, Sergipe, Pará and Amazonas joined. The legalist forces retook São
  Paulo by late July; the rebels withdrew toward the interior and the Paraná
  River. (Chapter I, book pp. 4–6.)

### 1924–1925 — the South and the column

- Prestes was serving with the 1st Railway Battalion at Santo Ângelo. The
  state-wide revolt began on 28–29 October 1924. Rebels took Santo Ângelo,
  São Borja and Uruguaiana; Juarez Távora was among the leaders at Uruguaiana.
  (Chapter II, book pp. 43–48.)
- Government forces converged on São Luiz Gonzaga. Prestes avoided the
  encirclement and marched north. Macaulay places the 3 January 1925 fight
  **near Palmeiras**, not under the name Ramada; the column lost about forty men
  there. It reached the Uruguay River on 9 January and crossed near the mouth of
  the Rio das Antas later in January, losing most of its horses and continuing
  on foot through Santa Catarina. (Chapter II, book p. 61; chapter III,
  book pp. 74–76.) The name "Ramada" and the date are confirmed by
  `revolucao-cultural-2025`; record the toponymic difference instead of
  silently renaming Macaulay's Palmeiras.
- The southern and São Paulo forces did **not** join at Foz do Iguaçu. Prestes
  and Miguel Costa met at **Benjamin Constant do Paraná on 3 April 1925**; they
  agreed to combine and move to Porto Santa Helena. They then went to Foz do
  Iguaçu to confer with Marshal Isidoro, who was the overall commander and
  remained at the political headquarters. The First Revolutionary Division was
  formally organized on **14 April 1925**, and the crossing ran from Porto
  Mendes to Puerto Adela: the landing began on 27 April, the crossing was
  completed on 29 April. Macaulay gives about 1,500 men, 600 horses and mules,
  and three 75-mm guns. Prestes' Rio Grande Brigade had roughly 800 men; the
  São Paulo Brigade roughly 700. (Chapter III, book pp. 86–89.)
- Miguel Costa was the division's formal commander; Prestes was chief of staff
  and the column's most visible leader. (Chapter III, book pp. 88–89, and
  Foreword.)

### The march

- The column wandered for more than two years and about 25,000 kilometers,
  through the pampas, the Paraná forests, the Northeast interior, the central
  savannas and the Mato Grosso wetlands, reaching the Bolivian and Paraguayan
  borders. (Foreword, book pp. x–xi.)
- The frontispiece map divides the march at Serra Nova for cartographic
  convenience:
  - São Luiz Gonzaga, Rio Grande do Sul → Serra Nova, Minas Gerais
    (December 1924–April 1926), solid line.
  - Serra Nova, Minas Gerais → San Matias, Bolivia
    (April 1926–February 1927), dashed line.
- The map periodization is not a claim that a "second phase" began at Serra
  Nova. Macaulay's text places a **rearguard attack at Serra Nova on 22 April
  1926** by the jagunços of Horácio de Matos and Abílio Wolney; the column broke
  contact and then made the decision to emigrate. (Chapter VIII, book pp. 220–221.)
  The ALMG/Nascimento account adds the federal attempt to encircle the column
  there and the "laço húngaro" maneuver; Taiobeiras was occupied on 26 April.
- The Verdinho action is chapter V, book pp. 122–124: Prestes and Cordeiro's
  detachment at the bridge on the afternoon of 27 June 1925; the bridge was
  burned, and the rearguard waded the shallow river on 28 June. Klinger's
  pursuers later cut a ford with the bridge timbers.
- The Pantanal crossing is chapter IX, book pp. 230–234. The main column entered
  Bolivia on 3 February 1927; Siqueira Campos's group crossed into Paraguay in
  March 1927. Macaulay does **not** mention La Gaiba or a 5 July 1927 monument.
  Use `blanc-2024`, ch. 7 and fig. 7.5, for the La Gaiba monument and its
  inscription; the exact dedication date remains secondary.
- The map is a reference for the campaign route; the game will recreate it as an
  original low-poly map, not reuse the scan.

## Use in the game

- **Prologue:** the Forte de Copacabana and the 1922 failure; Prestes is absent.
- **“O Anel de Ferro”:** Santo Ângelo, São Luiz Gonzaga, the breakout and Ramada.
- **Campaign map:** the two-phase route, with the interior as the main space.
- **Command:** Miguel Costa's formal command and Prestes' chief-of-staff role
  shape briefings, dialogue and mission perspective.
- **Counterpoint:** Blanc supplies the social/interior layer; Amado supplies
  tone; Macaulay supplies the chronological spine.
